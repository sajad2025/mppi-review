# Preface

Model Predictive Path Integral control, MPPI for short, is a way of controlling a system by simulating it many times. At every time step the controller draws a few hundred or a few thousand random control sequences, simulates what each would do, scores each simulated trajectory with a cost, and applies a weighted average of the sequences in which low-cost trajectories count for more. Then it repeats from the new state.

The method needs no derivatives of the dynamics or of the cost, it handles discontinuous costs such as collision penalties, and the simulations are independent, so they run in parallel on a graphics processor. These three properties explain most of its popularity in robotics since about 2016.

The algorithm fits in twenty lines. Understanding why those lines are the right ones takes longer, because the justification draws on four subjects that are usually taught separately: optimal control, model predictive control, Monte Carlo estimation, and a small amount of information theory. This book develops each of them from the beginning, to the depth MPPI needs and no further, and then assembles them.

## Who this is for

A reader who knows linear algebra, multivariable calculus and basic probability (expectation, the Gaussian distribution) can follow every chapter. A first course in control helps but is not assumed. Chapters 6 and 7 use stochastic differential equations; they can be skipped on a first reading, because Chapter 8 derives the same algorithm in discrete time with elementary tools.

## How the book is organised

| Chapters | Subject |

| 11 | Tuning and practice |
| 12 to 14 | Related methods, variants, and what is proved about MPPI |

The development follows the standard sources. Dynamic programming follows Bertsekas; model predictive control follows Rawlings, Mayne and Diehl; stochastic control follows Fleming and Soner; Monte Carlo follows Owen; path integral control follows Kappen and Theodorou; and MPPI itself follows the papers of Williams and co-authors, whose notation is used wherever possible. Full citations are in Chapter 16.

## Notation

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$ | state at time step $t$ |
| $u_t \in \mathbb{R}^m$ | control (the mean of what is applied) |
| $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ | control noise |
| $v_t = u_t + \varepsilon_t$ | perturbed control actually applied in a simulation |
| $F$ | discrete-time dynamics, $x_{t+1} = F(x_t, v_t)$ |
| $T$ | horizon (number of time steps) |
| $K$ | number of sampled trajectories (rollouts) |
| $U, V, \mathcal{E}$ | the sequences $(u_0,\dots,u_{T-1})$, $(v_0,\dots,v_{T-1})$, $(\varepsilon_0,\dots,\varepsilon_{T-1})$ |
| $q(x)$, $\phi(x)$ | running state cost and terminal cost |
| $S(V)$ | state cost of the trajectory generated by $V$ |
| $\lambda \gt 0$ | temperature |
| $w_k$ | importance weight of rollout $k$ |
| $\mathbb{P}$, $\mathbb{Q}$ | distributions over control sequences, with densities $p$, $q$ |
| $\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{Q} \parallel \mathbb{P})$ | relative entropy (Kullback–Leibler divergence) |

Vectors are columns, $A^\top$ is the transpose, and $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\cdot]$ is an expectation when the random quantity has distribution $\mathbb{Q}$.
